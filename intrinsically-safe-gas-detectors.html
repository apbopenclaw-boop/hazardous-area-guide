Intrinsically safe gas detectors for Zone 1 & Zone 2 hazardous areas

An intrinsically safe gas detector is a portable monitoring device certified to operate in explosive atmospheres. This guide covers single-gas and multi-gas options, compares leading models from MSA, Drager, Honeywell, and Crowcon, and explains the certification and maintenance requirements.

What makes a gas detector intrinsically safe

Portable gas detectors for hazardous areas use the intrinsic safety (Ex i) protection concept, defined in IEC 60079-11. The device's electrical circuits — including the electrochemical or catalytic sensors, the battery, the display, and the audible/visual alarm — are all designed to operate at energy levels too low to ignite the surrounding atmosphere.

This is the same protection concept used in intrinsically safe two-way radios and certified flashlights. The key difference is that gas detectors must also maintain sensor accuracy while operating within the energy constraints — a catalytic bead sensor requires a heated element to detect combustible gases, and this heat source must be carefully managed within the Ex i limits.

Most portable gas detectors carry Ex ia IIC T4 Ga certification — the highest intrinsic safety level, covering Zone 0 (continuous explosive atmosphere). This means they can be used anywhere: Zone 0, Zone 1, and Zone 2. Equipment rated Ex ia is safe even with two simultaneous faults, making it suitable for the most demanding applications including confined space entry. For more on the gas group classification system that determines which detector covers which gases, see exknowledge.com.

Single-gas vs multi-gas detectors

Single-gas detectors monitor one specific gas — most commonly hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), carbon monoxide (CO), or oxygen (O₂). They are compact (typically under 100g), inexpensive, and require minimal training. Some models are disposable with a fixed 2-year sensor life, eliminating calibration requirements entirely.

Multi-gas detectors (typically 4-gas or 5-gas) monitor multiple hazards simultaneously. The standard 4-gas configuration is LEL (combustible gas), O₂ (oxygen depletion/enrichment), H₂S, and CO. Five-gas models add a PID (photoionisation detector) sensor for volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Multi-gas detectors are heavier (200–350g) and more expensive, but provide comprehensive protection and are required by most confined space entry procedures.

For routine area monitoring where only one hazard is present (e.g., H₂S in an upstream oil and gas environment), single-gas detectors are practical and cost-effective. For petrochemical plants, refineries, and any operation involving confined space entry, multi-gas detectors are the standard.

Calibration and bump testing

Gas detector calibration does not affect the Ex certification — the intrinsic safety of the device is independent of sensor accuracy. However, an uncalibrated detector may fail to alarm at the correct gas concentration, which is a safety and regulatory issue.

Bump testing exposes the detector to a known concentration of calibration gas to verify the sensors respond and the alarms activate. Most sites perform bump tests before each shift or at least daily. Automated bump test stations (docking stations) from MSA, Drager, and Honeywell reduce this to a 30-second process per detector.

Full calibration adjusts the sensor readings to match a certified reference gas concentration. Manufacturer recommendations typically specify 180-day calibration intervals. Some jurisdictions and operators require shorter intervals (90 days). Disposable single-gas detectors with pre-calibrated sealed sensors eliminate this requirement entirely — when the sensor expires, the entire unit is replaced.

Connected gas detection

The latest generation of intrinsically safe gas detectors includes cellular or mesh-network connectivity that transmits real-time gas readings to a central safety dashboard. MSA's Safety io platform, Honeywell's Connected Worker, and Crowcon's Connect system all offer cloud-based monitoring with automated compliance reporting.

Connected detectors retain their Ex ia certification — the wireless module is assessed as part of the intrinsic safety evaluation. The connectivity adds value for lone worker monitoring, emergency mustering, and centralised calibration tracking. The trade-off is higher unit cost and shorter battery life due to the radio transmitter's power consumption.

FAQ

What is an intrinsically safe gas detector?

An intrinsically safe gas detector is a portable monitoring device certified to operate in explosive atmospheres without causing ignition. It limits energy in all circuits — sensors, battery, display, alarm — so no spark can ignite surrounding gases.

Do I need a multi-gas or single-gas detector?

Single-gas detectors are lighter and cheaper, suitable when only one hazard is present. Multi-gas detectors (4-gas: LEL, O₂, H₂S, CO) provide broader protection and are required for confined space entry.

How often must intrinsically safe gas detectors be calibrated?

Most manufacturers recommend every 180 days. Many sites bump test before each shift. Disposable single-gas models eliminate calibration entirely — the unit is replaced when the sensor expires.
